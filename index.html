    A second lens on the same question

    The Monte Carlo run above answers "given how this program has actually performed, what's the range of honest outcomes?" — it forward-simulates from this ledger's own observed efficiency. Reference class forecasting asks a different question: "how have similar completed programs, elsewhere, actually turned out?" Bent Flyvbjerg's database of 16,000+ megaprojects found rail projects run 45% over budget on average, fixed-link (bridge/tunnel) projects 34%, and roads 20% — overruns that are prevalent and predictable, not random noise. The same database puts the "trifecta" rate — on budget, on schedule, and delivering the promised benefits, all three — at roughly 0.5% (80 of 15,920 projects analyzed). That is the honest denominator a program-level forecast is actually competing against. The two techniques are complementary, not competing: one grounds a forecast in this program's own track record, the other checks it against the base rate for programs of this type. A forecast that only does the first can still be optimistic in a way an entire asset class already tells you to expect. (Bent Flyvbjerg's published megaproject research, via PMI's research summary, verified 21 Aug 2026 — a real, external citation, not this program's own data.)

    Float is the early-warning system

    DCMA

    Cost variance tells you what already happened; total float tells you what is about to. Negative float means the package is driving the program completion date — recovery anywhere else does not move it.

    Total float by package (working days)

    Critical Path Length Index

    CPLI = (remaining duration + total float) ÷ remaining duration. Below 1.00 the path cannot absorb any more slip; DCMA flags below 0.95.

    How this is actually computed

    Milestone variance

    Why SPI alone will mislead you

    Schedule Performance Index is measured in dollars of earned value, not days. It drifts back toward 1.00 as a program completes — even one finishing late — because earned value converges on budget regardless of when the work lands. It also cannot distinguish work on the critical path from float-rich work.

    The direct fix is SPI(t) — Earned Schedule translated into the same efficiency-ratio shape as SPI, but measured in time instead of dollars, so it never mathematically converges to 1.00 just because the budget eventually gets spent. This framework pairs it with CPLI (is the driving path still achievable?) and BEI (are activities actually completing on plan?) — three numbers, three different failure modes, none of them fooled by spend rate alone.

    A real, verified requirement behind this DCMA framing

    Sound Transit's own 2023 Standard Division 01 Specifications require prime contractors to schedule in Oracle Primavera P6 — Section 01 32 13.25, “Scheduling of Work” (the sub-$10M design-bid-build variant; sibling sections 01 32 13.10/.15/.20 cover larger and design-build contracts). This tab's own DCMA-style logic — CPLI, BEI, float erosion — is built from the same real methodology those specifications require, independently re-verified directly against the primary specification document, not cited secondhand. (Sound Transit 2023 Standard Division 01 Specifications, verified 18 Aug 2026 — a real, external citation, not this program's own data.)

    Named precisely, not just “DCMA-style”: the DCMA 14-Point Assessment is the specific schedule-health audit this dashboard's CPLI and BEI cards implement — checks 13 and 14 of that 14-point methodology, by definition, not a paraphrase of it. The 14-Point Assessment itself sits under the broader ANSI/EIA-748 Earned Value Management System standard federal and major transit capital programs are built to comply with. The other 12 checks — missing logic ties, negative lag, hard constraints, invalid dates, and the rest — audit a real CPM activity network with logic ties between tasks; this dashboard works from aggregate float and duration per control account, not an activity-level schedule file, so those 12 stay a named, honest gap rather than something faked from data this ledger doesn't carry.

    Delivery health

    productivity · document control · quality

    Cost and schedule indices are lagging indicators — by the time CPI moves, the money is spent. These are the leading ones: they move weeks earlier and they are the levers a controls manager can actually pull.

    Productivity factor by package

    Earned hours ÷ actual hours. Below 1.00 means the crews are burning more hours than the estimate carried — visible weeks before it reaches CPI.

    Document control aging

    RFI and submittal turnaround are the earliest signals of a design-phase problem, and the most common root cause of a later claim.

    Safety & compliance

    The one metric that can stop the job independent of cost or schedule — and its trend moves with acceleration pressure.

    Funding vs. spend

    Grant drawdown should track expenditure. Spending ahead of drawdown is a cash-flow exposure; drawing ahead of spend is a compliance one.

    Field-to-boardroom cascade

    one root cause, five instruments

    The Schedule tab already says it in one sentence: the same tunnel ground condition shows up in the priced risk register, a quality NCR, a crew's weekly cost-per-hour, the Critical Float Erosion Rate and the schedule-drift trend — one root cause showing up in five different instruments. This walks four of those into where it actually lands: Field Friction (risk register + NCR) → Delivery Impact (crew CPH) → Schedule Impact (float) → Gate 5's own governance hard stop.

    Quality NCR register

    A rising ratio of unresolved Non-Conformance Reports is a leading indicator of downstream rework and delayed systems testing — standard project-controls doctrine, not a program-specific claim: relying on contractor-asserted quality status instead of independently tracked NCR aging is exactly how a physical defect keeps propagating after it's already been caught on paper. Read live off the same Actions/RAID register every other tab's stale-flag and escalation logic already reads — not a separate register to keep in sync.

    Crew cost-per-hour

    weekly · leading indicator

    Cost per hour translates a crew's labor, equipment and overhead into a burn rate and checks it weekly against a standard — the same discipline behind CPH reporting on a manufacturing shop floor, applied to a field crew. It surfaces a problem days before it shows up in the control account's own CPI, and because it is tracked weekly rather than monthly, it can isolate why the burn rate moved, not just that it did.

    Data pipeline · guardrails · AI narrative

    How the numbers get here — and why leadership can trust them

    Every figure on this dashboard traces to a raw record through SQL anyone can re-run. The pipeline is deterministic (same rows in, same ledger out), guarded by checks that fail the build before a bad number can travel, and the AI layer writes prose only — it never touches a number.

    Pipeline architecture

    This is not a drawing of a hypothetical. The same model runs in this repository: pipeline/run_pipeline.py synthesizes the raw monthly claims deterministically, builds the ledger through fct_control_account.sql in DuckDB, and proves the result identical to what this page computes in JavaScript — 64 checks, two independent stacks, one set of numbers. That equality is the integrity argument.

    Follow one box through the pipeline

    The model — fct_control_account.sql

    with monthly as (
  select package_id,
         sum(pv_delta) as pv,
         sum(ev_delta) as ev,
         sum(ac_delta) as ac
  from stg_progress_claims
  where claim_month <= date '2026-07-31'  -- data date, explicit
  group by package_id
)
select m.package_id, b.bac, m.pv, m.ev, m.ac,
       m.ev - m.pv                     as sv,
       m.ev - m.ac                     as cv,
       m.ev / nullif(m.pv, 0)          as spi,
       m.ev / nullif(m.ac, 0)          as cpi,
       b.bac / (m.ev / nullif(m.ac,0)) as eac,
       b.bac - ...                     as vac
from monthly m
join dim_control_account b using (package_id)

    The guardrails — schema.yml (dbt tests)

    models:
  - name: stg_progress_claims
    columns:
      - name: claim_id
        tests: [unique, not_null]
      - name: package_id
        tests: [not_null, relationships]
      - name: pv_delta / ev_delta / ac_delta
        tests: [non-negative]      # value never flows backward

  - name: fct_control_account
    tests:
      - expression_is_true: ev <= bac   # cannot earn
                                        # more than budget
      - expression_is_true: pct <= 1
      - spi between 0.5 and 1.5            # outside this, the
                                           # inputs are broken,
                                           # not the project

    Integrity gate — running now, in this page

    These are not screenshots of checks. This page executes them against the live ledger on every load; a single failure turns the gate red. The same invariants run as dbt tests in the pipeline and as assertions in stress.cjs — three enforcement points for one contract.

    Statistical control — retrospective anomaly check

    The guards above are deterministic tie-outs — recompute and compare, pass or fail. This is a different kind of check: a statistical control run over a real historical series already on this page (CP-201's weekly crew cost-per-hour), flagging any point beyond ±2.5 standard deviations from the series mean — a standard SPC parameter choice, not derived from this program's own history (same disclosure the EWMA panel below makes about its own λ/L). Not a live feed — this ledger is static and synthetic, same as everywhere else on this page — but the same rule that would catch an anomalous ERP posting on a real, continuously-updating series, run honestly against the one real (if small) series this dashboard has.

    How this is actually computed

    EWMA control chart — catching persistent drift, not just outliers

    The check above flags any single point beyond ±2.5 standard deviations of the series mean — blind to a small, persistent drift that never individually crosses that line. An Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA) tracks the whole trend instead, weighting recent points more heavily, so a steady creep still shows up even when no single week is itself an outlier. Same real series as the check above (CP-201's weekly crew cost-per-hour) — a second, complementary lens on it, not a replacement.

    How this is actually computed

    Ingestion validation — not the same thing as the gate above

    The integrity gate above re-derives already-accepted totals and compares them — reconciliation, not validation. This checks something earlier: whether a raw record should have been trusted at all before it entered the ledger. A real predictive-controls engine quarantines a bad record at intake — negative actual costs, physical progress claimed past 100%, task updates posted after the data date, costs charged to an unmapped cost code, schedule imports carrying open-ended tasks or hard constraints on the critical path, and baseline changes without a signed change-order ID. Only the first two are honestly checkable against this static ledger — the other four need a live schedule/ERP feed this demo doesn't have and never will; naming that gap here instead of quietly skipping it.

    AI narrative — under a verification contract

    The contract: the model proposes, the code disposes. An LLM drafting the monthly narrative receives computed figures and writes only prose; before anything renders, every number in the draft is re-extracted and recomputed from the ledger by an independent path, and anything unverifiable blocks publication. This demo runs the identical contract with deterministic templates standing in for the model — the guardrail does not change either way.

    Beyond this program · the real problem, not the synthetic one

    Connecting scattered, unclean, real-world data

    Grounded in the real AEC industry standard — ISO 19650 Common Data Environments, buildingSMART's IDS — not generic tech-industry terms, this tab is the plan for the real problem: a capital program's data lives across six-plus systems that were never designed to agree with each other, and someone has to stage it, guard it, resolve disagreement between systems, and recover from errors proactively instead of just flagging them. Everything else on this dashboard runs against one clean, synthetic ledger — a real program doesn't have that luxury.

    The problem, stated plainly

    There's a specific, well-documented version of this: the Work Breakdown Structure (WBS) contractors use for project controls, and the Asset Breakdown Structure (ABS) transit agencies use for operations and maintenance, are organized around fundamentally different logic — work packages and spatial boundaries versus functional system topology. That mismatch is a documented, recurring cause of data loss at handover, not a one-off integration bug. The failure mode is rarely one dramatic error. It's the slow accumulation of small disagreements nobody resolved, until a report reaches a Board built on numbers that quietly stopped meaning the same thing across systems. The goal isn't “connect the systems” — it's making disagreement between systems something the process catches and resolves, not something a report silently averages over.

    Architecture — a governed staging pipeline, not a direct feed

    The AEC industry's own real, named version of "stage before trust" is the Common Data Environment (CDE) under ISO 19650 — four defined states, each requiring a specific approval gate before information can advance. The rule that matters most isn't the four states themselves — it's that nothing downstream ever reads a raw source directly, and nothing advances without its gate. Drawn below as a flow, with the discrepancy-resolution loop from "When systems disagree" (further down this tab) folded in at the point it actually happens — the verification gate into Shared.

    Automated guardrails — machine-readable, not just described

    A real, small-scale version of buildingSMART's Information Delivery Specification (IDS) — an XML standard pairing applicability (which records a rule governs) with requirements (what those records must have) so a submittal that fails gets rejected with a specific, itemized reason. This dashboard's own 28-check integrity gate is a working small-scale version of the same pattern.
