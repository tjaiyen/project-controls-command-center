You know what a job should cost when you bid it

The open question is whether you know what it actually cost while there is still time to do something about it. I spent four and a half years in Mukilteo owning the first half of that sentence — takeoffs, unit rates, margin, and the GC proposal that came out the other end. I spent the two years after it owning the second half: production cost across four Collins Aerospace sites as the SAP CO/PP/MM/FICO subject‑matter expert, then building B.E. Meyers & Co.'s entire cost and forecasting infrastructure from zero as its first dedicated cost accountant. The bid side and the actuals side are the same problem seen from opposite ends, and most people who do project controls have only ever stood at one end of it.

The structural argument: a unitized curtain wall job is not a construction schedule with a factory attached — it is a production line with an installation window. The shop thinks in panels and takt; the field thinks in floors and crew days; accounting thinks in cost codes. One job number, three languages. That is a manufacturing‑controls problem wearing a construction hat, and it is the seam my two careers actually sit on.

One thing I checked before writing this

Your own site says Walters & Wolf is “disciplined in practicing the Toyota production system, growing and building lean thinkers” — alongside a stated library of over six thousand continuous‑improvement videos. A shop that already thinks in flow, pull, takt and buffers deserves project controls that speak that language. Almost no controls candidate does; most will hand you a critical‑path model and call the factory an input. The metrics I would build for you — panel flow, days of buffer cover, shop‑versus‑field cost crosswalk — are lean metrics that happen to roll up into earned value, not the other way round. (waltersandwolf.com/how-we-do-it, verified 2 Sep 2026)

2What I don't bring, stated once

The scheduling half, without the hedging

Nearly every partial and the one outright gap above trace to a single thing. Here it is plainly, because finding it out in the fifth meeting would be worse for both of us.

Gap  Owning a CPM schedule in Primavera P6

Zero. Not “limited exposure” — I have never owned a P6 model and I do not claim otherwise on a resume, in a survey, or here. The same is true of Deltek Cobra and Costpoint. If what you need is a scheduler to administer a critical‑path model, I am the wrong call.

What I would say about it: P6 is a bounded, learnable tool. Structured fundamentals run a few days to a working command of the interface; genuine schedule ownership — logic ties, resource levelling, baseline management — builds over the following months on real project data. That is the same ramp any new CPM user faces. I am not going to dress it up as anything shorter.

3Your operation, specifically

Six places margin leaks on unitized work

Independent of whether a system exists to see them in, these are structural to unitized work and worth naming. I am not claiming they are all true in Mukilteo today — I have been gone since January 2024 and I would be guessing. Any single one of them is worth more than the fee below; the point of a first engagement is finding out which ones you actually have, and building the first piece of the thing that lets you see them.

The backdrop, so the leak list below isn't read in a vacuum

The commercial office market that built the branch's historical volume hasn't paused — it has reset. Downtown Seattle office vacancy hit a record 35.8% in Q2 2026 and the regional office construction pipeline is down roughly 99% from its 2023 peak. (Kidder Mathews, Q2 2026) Meanwhile a different kind of building envelope has become the most heavily financed segment in commercial construction: North American data‑center inventory grew 33.7% year‑over‑year to a record 10,903 MW in H1 2026, with vacancy at a record‑low 1.4%. (CBRE, H1 2026) Grant County, one Cascade crossing east, is mid‑way through a $260M program to roughly double its deliverable power by 2029. (Grant PUD QTEP)

I've been gone since January 2024, so which of those trendlines matters more to the branch's 2026 plan isn't mine to call from outside. What doesn't change either way: when the volume you're chasing tightens or shifts, the operation that already knows its real job cost — panel by panel, vendor by vendor — makes that call faster and with less guessing than the one re‑deriving it under pressure. That is the case for what follows, independent of which market the branch is building into next.

1 · Shop and field roll up to one margin in different units
Panels fabricated versus floors set. Without a crosswalk, “how are we doing” has no single answer.
2 · Percent complete is contested
The GC says one number, the field says another, accounting says a third. On unitized work physical completion is genuinely countable — panels set over panels total — which is a luxury stick‑built jobs do not have. It only helps if someone tracks it that way.
3 · The buffer is real but invisible
Everyone knows you want crated units ahead of the setting crew. Almost nobody carries a days‑of‑cover number that trends, so buffer depletion is noticed when the crane goes idle rather than three weeks earlier.
4 · RFIs and change orders are managed as correspondence, not as priced exposure
Procore holds the thread; the aging pipeline of unpriced change rarely shows up as a dollar figure against margin until it settles.
5 · Rework from tolerance drift books late
Small setting variances compound across an elevation sweep. By the time it is a perimeter joint that will not take sealant, the cost is already spent.
6 · Estimating re-derives price it already paid for once
Without a queryable history of what a product cost, from which vendor, on what kind of job, every bid starts closer to zero than it needs to.

Why I can write that list without a discovery call

Because I priced this work for four and a half years. I know what a split‑mullion stack joint is, why the crating order is reversed against the setting sweep, what the starter sill flood test gates before panels go above it, and what it costs when a takeoff and a material schedule quietly disagree — because I found one that did. Linking unit geometry directly to the material schedule reconciled two disconnected takeoffs, surfaced a real square‑footage discrepancy, and turned addenda repricing from days into minutes. That is the seed of the first engagement below — the same discipline, applied to your vendor quote and PO history instead of one takeoff.

4Where I'd start

Three shapes, and the one I'd pick

All three are on the page rather than assuming which fits your year. The recommendation is the first one, because it is the shape where you find out whether I am useful before committing to anything.

A · Estimating History Assembly $4,000–$8,000 fixed · 2–4 weeks Recommended
Assemble your scattered bid quotes and past purchase orders — email, folders, PDFs, whatever form they are actually in — into one queryable price history, coded to CSI MasterFormat 08 44 13 so it stays portable into whatever you use next. You get the history itself, plus a written finding on what it shows about your last several bids: where price moved against you, which vendors drift from quote to final PO, and where a repeat product is priced inconsistently for no visible reason. Yours to keep whether or not anything follows. This is the smallest possible closed loop — it touches no PM's process and needs no system to exist first, which is deliberate.
B · The shared substrate $8,000–$15,000 build · $1,000–$2,500/mo
For when the answer to A is “we want this live, and we want the rest of it too.” Not a dashboard — a small, versioned set of handoff templates (cost breakdown, schedule of values, PO, change order) that behave like the Excel your PMs already keep, feeding one governed data layer underneath. No PM is asked to stop using Excel or adopt anyone else's method — the standard is the shape of the handoff, not the tool. What comes back to each PM is something none of them can produce alone: their job against the last several of its type. I would want A done first regardless.
C · Fractional, embedded, or salaried $175–225/hr, or W‑2
A set number of days a month owning controls across the branch's active jobs, convertible to a salaried seat if it earns one. I am open on the shape and would rather you pick it than have me presume.

What I'd need from you

  • Whatever bid quotes and past POs exist, in whatever form and however scattered — that is the raw material for Option A, not a precondition for it.
  • Thirty minutes each with an estimator, a PM, and whoever owns the shop schedule.
  • One person who can tell me when I am wrong about how the operation runs today, and who can speak for what your PMs would and would not accept — ideally the person who will actually lead this once it is running.
  • If there turns out to be an ERP or a controls tool already in place, tell me — it changes nothing about Option A and changes the shape of Option B.
